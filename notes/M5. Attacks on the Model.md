# M5. Attacks on the Model

M4 ended with models the broker runs and offers to others. Each one answers questions, and every answer tells an attacker something about the model. With enough answers, an attacker can find inputs the model gets wrong, build a copy of it, or work out who was in its training data, all without touching a file or a server.

## The idea

Most of the course attacked the system around the model: the prompt, the tools, the data it reads. This note attacks the model itself, through the only thing an outside attacker gets: its outputs. These attacks are older than chatbots. They were studied on image classifiers and fraud models years before prompt injection had a name, and they apply to every model the broker runs.

The broker has two models that face outsiders:

- **The order-surveillance classifier.** It scores every order for signs of manipulation, such as a burst of orders placed and cancelled to move a price. A high score holds the order for a person to review. Mallory cannot see the score, but she can see whether her order was held.
- **The fine-tuned reply model from M2,** which partners can now call through the broker's API (note 6a), and which was trained on client chats.

Four attacks, from the least access needed to the most:

| Attack | What the attacker wants | What they need | ATLAS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evasion | Inputs the model gets wrong | Answers to their own inputs | AML.T0015 Evade AI Model; AML.T0043 Craft Adversarial Data |
| Model extraction | A copy of the model | Many answers, ideally with scores | AML.T0024.002 Extract AI Model; AML.T0005 Create Proxy AI Model |
| Membership inference | Whether a given record was in the training data | Answers, ideally with scores, for that record | AML.T0024.000 Infer Training Data Membership |
| Inversion and training-data extraction | The training data itself | Answers; the weights help | AML.T0024.001 Invert AI Model |

In AppSec terms, the model's API is an oracle, like a login form that says "wrong password" differently from "no such user". What it reveals, and how often it can be asked, decide what an attacker can learn.

This note is explanation with sources.

## Step 1: evasion

In 2013, Szegedy and others showed that changes to an image too small for a person to notice could make a neural network classify it as something else entirely. Goodfellow and others (2014) explained why and gave a fast way to find such changes. These **adversarial examples** are the original attack on models, and prompt injection is a version of it for language models: an input made to get a model to do something its owner did not intend.

For the surveillance classifier, Mallory does not need small pixel changes. She needs orders that achieve her manipulation and score below the threshold. She can search for them:

1. Place small orders in different patterns: sizes, timings, cancel rates.
2. Note which ones are held. Each held or not-held result is one answer from the model.
3. Keep changing the pattern towards what gets through, then scale it up.

This is the "attacker moves second" problem from P5, against a classifier instead of a guardrail. Any model used as a security control can be probed this way, including note 10a's injection classifier.

Controls:

- **Keep a hard limit outside the model.** Some patterns are blocked by rules in code whatever the model says, such as a maximum cancel rate per account per minute. The model catches what the rules miss. It is never the only check, in the same way `check_order` does not depend on the reply model.
- **Reveal as little as possible.** The held-or-not outcome cannot be hidden, but the score, the reasons and the threshold can.
- **Watch for probing.** An account that keeps landing just below the threshold, or tries many small variations, is a signal in itself, like note 14's monitoring signals.
- **Retrain with what got through.** Each evasion found by the red team or in production becomes a training and test example, as each successful attack became an eval case in note 10.

## Step 2: model extraction

In 2016, Tramèr and others showed that models offered through prediction APIs could be copied closely by sending queries and training a new model on the answers. Scores and probabilities in the answers made it much cheaper.

Language models are not exempt. Carlini and others (ICML 2024) recovered part of the internals of production models through their normal APIs: the whole final projection layer of two OpenAI models for under $20, and the exact hidden size of gpt-3.5-turbo. Their attack used details the APIs returned, such as token probabilities. The broader version is **distillation**: training your own model on another model's outputs. Providers' terms generally forbid using their outputs to build competing models, which shows how real they consider the risk.

Why the broker cares:

- **The fine-tuned model is an asset.** It holds the broker's style and domain knowledge, paid for with M2's data work.
- **A copy is a test bench.** With a local copy of the surveillance classifier, Mallory can search for evasions offline, without any held orders to warn the broker, then use them against the real one. Evasions often transfer from a copy to the original (ATLAS calls this Black-Box Transfer, AML.T0043.002). Extraction makes Step 1 quiet.

Controls:

- **Return labels, not scores.** The partner API returns the reply text, without token probabilities. Internal models return a decision, without a confidence.
- **Quotas per client and per partner,** like note 10's budgets, sized for real use and not for millions of queries.
- **Watch the shape of the queries.** Extraction needs inputs that cover the space systematically, which look different from real use.
- **Contract terms** that forbid training on the outputs, and a way to revoke a partner's access (note 12).

## Step 3: membership inference

In 2017, Shokri and others showed that an attacker with only query access could often tell whether a particular record had been in a model's training data. Models tend to be more confident on examples they were trained on, and that difference can be measured.

For large language models trained on huge datasets, the picture is more mixed. Duan and others (2024) tested five membership attacks on models trained on a large public dataset and found them barely better than guessing, because the dataset was so large, each example was seen only about once, and the line between seen and unseen text was blurry. A later study found that stronger attacks do better than chance, but still with limited success.

That result should not reassure the broker, because its own model is the opposite case. M2's fine-tune was trained on a small set of client chats, possibly for several passes. Small data and repeated passes are exactly the conditions under which membership attacks work. And membership itself can be the secret: "this chat came from a client who complained about a mis-sold product" or "this account was in the flagged-accounts training set" are things the broker must not reveal.

## Step 4: inversion and training-data extraction

Inversion goes further: it reconstructs training data, or features of it, from the model.

- Fredrikson and others (2015) reconstructed recognisable images of people's faces from a face recognition model, given only their names and access to the model's confidence scores.
- Carlini and others (2021) extracted verbatim training text from GPT-2, including names and contact details (M2).
- Nasr and others (2023) made ChatGPT repeat one word until its output broke down, after which some of its output was copied from training data, at a rate about 150 times higher than normal. Safety training had not removed the memorised text.
- Note 8 showed the same idea for embeddings: text can be largely recovered from its embedding.

For the broker, this is M2's warning made concrete: whatever went into the fine-tune can come back out to a partner, through the API, years later. M2's controls (scrub before training, leave out real holdings, retrain to delete) are the main defence. Note 10a's output filters for PAN patterns are a second layer on the way out.

## Step 5: differential privacy

**Differential privacy** is a guarantee about training: the trained model would be almost the same whether or not any one person's data had been included. If that holds, the model cannot reveal much about any one person, because it barely depends on them. How "almost the same" is set by a number called the privacy budget (epsilon). Smaller is more private.

The usual way to train with it is DP-SGD (Abadi and others, 2016): limit how much any one example can change the model at each training step, and add random noise to the changes. The cost is accuracy, and more privacy costs more accuracy. Google's VaultGemma (2025) is a one-billion-parameter model trained entirely with differential privacy. Google reported that its usefulness was comparable to non-private models from about five years earlier: real, and well behind current models.

For the broker:

- **It is the one defence with a proof** against membership inference and extraction of individual records, for the budget chosen. Everything else in this note lowers the rate.
- **It protects examples, not people, unless set up for people.** If one client appears in a hundred chats, protecting each chat separately is weaker than protecting the client. The unit of privacy has to be decided before training.
- **It does not stop evasion or model extraction.** It is about the training data, not the model's decisions.
- **For the M2 fine-tune, the simpler control comes first:** keep client data out of the training set. Differential privacy is for the cases where the data has to go in, such as a fraud model that must learn from real accounts.

## OWASP risks in this note

The codes come from the [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/A1.%20OWASP%20Top%2010%20for%20LLM%20Applications.md), 2026 edition. Several of these attacks predate language models, so the ATLAS IDs in the table above often fit them more closely than an OWASP category.

### LLM02: Sensitive Information Disclosure

- **Example:** A partner sends the fine-tuned model thousands of near-copies of typical complaint chats and compares its confidence, to work out which clients' complaints were in the training set.
- **Issue:** A small fine-tuning set, seen several times, makes membership visible, and membership itself is sensitive.
- **Fix:** Keep client data out of training (M2). Where it must go in, train with differential privacy at the client level, and return text without token probabilities.

### LLM06: Unbounded Consumption

- **Example:** A partner account sends two million systematic queries to the reply model over a month and trains its own copy on the answers.
- **Issue:** Nothing limited or watched the volume or shape of the queries, and the broker paid for every one.
- **Fix:** Quotas per partner sized for real use, alerts on systematic query patterns, contract terms against training on outputs, and revocable access (note 12).

### LLM01: Prompt Injection

- **Example:** The red team probes note 10a's injection classifier with variations until one passes, then uses it against the assistant.
- **Issue:** A classifier used as a control is an evasion target, like any model.
- **Fix:** Treat the classifier as a soft control behind hard ones, watch for probing, and add each evasion to its training and test sets.

## End of Part M

The assistant's models now get the same treatment as the rest of its code, and the team knows how they fail. Downloaded files are data-only formats, pinned and first loaded in isolation (M1). Training data has an owner, a review and a hash (M2). Hosted models are pinned snapshots, gateways are locked dependencies, and every model, dataset and provider is listed in one place (M3). The team knows that roles and refusals are trained habits and that output is sampled (M4), and that a model's answers can be used to evade it, copy it or recover its training data (M5). Every one of these ends in the same rule as the main course: the model's behaviour lowers the risk, and code decides.
